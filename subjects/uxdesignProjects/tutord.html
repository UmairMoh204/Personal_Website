Tutor Scheduling Flow — Designing the Admin Experience for K–12 STEM Tutoring

I was tasked with designing TutorD’s admin experience so school admins could roster people, read classrooms, and act on reports without leaving the page they were on.

Quick Glance

01 - Assignment

TutorD is building thoughtful, adaptive STEM tutoring for K–12 students in urban settings. My team and I were asked to design the admin side — the people who roster classrooms and have to prove the product is working.

The client handed us a basic dashboard, user stories, and a teacher onboarding flow. The job was to turn that into an admin experience that could carry three jobs without sending someone hunting across pages.

Rostering

Add and remove admins, teachers, and students without breaking the classroom they belong to.

Classrooms

Give admins enough progress in the row that they do not have to click in to know how a class is doing.

Reports

Surface billing, engagement, and scores in a form an admin could actually show someone.

02 - Uncovering the Problem

The stakeholder interview was less about features and more about alignment. We needed to understand what the admin already had to build from and where the experience still needed structure.

What we inherited was a starting point, not a destination. The dashboard could hold people. It could not tell an admin, at a glance, whether a classroom was moving.

03 - Prioritizing Findings

We could not get into competitor admin pages, so the competitive audit looked at the surfaces we could actually open — and at jobs that overlapped with TutorD.

Schoology

Signs you into activity, not a blank hub. That pushed us to treat the classroom list as the place work starts.

Kahoot

Makes student performance the point of reports, not a buried export. We used that when we ranked what a report had to answer.

Quizlet

Shows progress as something you can scan, then open. That became the model for the classroom row.

We did not freeze a mid-fidelity and test it four times. After each of the four usability sessions we synthesized, changed the prototype, and tested the change. The alternative — one locked file — would have left the same pain points in place.

Student progress

Participants wanted a progression status for each student once they were looking at a classroom.

Unit statistics

They wanted quarter-to-quarter and year-to-year comparison, not a single score sitting alone.

Classroom year

They wanted the classroom page to show the current school year without a separate hunt.

How can admins see student progress, compare statistics,

and open the current school year without leaving the classroom view?

04 - Research to Design

We first drew the information architecture from our own mental model of an admin product. Usability tests and the competitive audit kept rewriting it. That was the point — the map had to follow what admins were trying to do, not the pages we had already drawn.

We synthesized each session into three buckets — usability, functionality, and changes — then wrote action items from the third. That kept “they didn’t like it” from dying as a note.

Admins needed more information in the classroom view. To solve this, I used progressive disclosure: show classroom progress in the row, then a hover percentile, then the full classroom year.

My pages were the classroom list, the organization page, and the navigation hover and dropdown states. Filling each classroom row — and putting a percentile on hover — was how I kept whitespace from becoming the thing an admin had to click through.

View Interactive Prototype

05 - Learning and Impact

A design system is also a map of where pages live

Aligning styles across the team was not only visual. It was how we agreed on where a page lived and how an admin got there, so new screens did not invent a second navigation.

Navigation has to be allowed to change

Every new page rewrote the nav. Treating that as a failure would have frozen a structure the product had already outgrown. Updating it was part of the design work.

Testing a moving prototype is how the row got dense enough

If we had waited to “finish” the mid-fidelity, the classroom row would still have been empty. Changing after each session is what put progress, hover, and year in the same view.
